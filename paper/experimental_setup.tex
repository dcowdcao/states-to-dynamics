\section{Experimental Setup}

Every experiment ran on NVIDIA H100 80GB GPUs. Training the sparse autoencoders takes 8--16
hours; every probe fit, rollout and read-out below is minutes on one GPU. The code is PyTorch
2.5.1 with TransformerLens 1.17.0 under Python 3.11.

\subsection{Model and activations}

We study OthelloGPT \citep{li2023othello}, an 8-layer decoder-only transformer trained to
predict the next legal move from a sequence of move tokens alone -- it never sees a board. We
use the TransformerLens port \texttt{Baidicoot/Othello-GPT-Transformer-Lens}: $d_{\rm model} =
512$, 8 heads of dimension 64, MLP width 2048, GELU, context length 59, vocabulary 61, 25.3M
parameters. It is loaded with TransformerLens defaults, so LayerNorm is folded into the
adjacent weights and the writing and unembedding weights are centered; every activation below
comes from that folded model. Token 0 is the pass token and tokens 1--60 are the playable
squares, which are the board minus the four center squares -- those start occupied and are
never played.

Unless stated otherwise activations are the residual stream after block 5,
\texttt{blocks.5.hook\_resid\_post}, so $z_t \in \mathbb{R}^{512}$. We read at layer 5 because
the board probe peaks there (0.9919, with layers 5--7 within 0.001 of each other). Position 0
is an attention sink whose norm is $\approx\!300$ against the $\sqrt{512} \approx 22.6$ of
every other position, so every trajectory measurement drops it: a game contributes 58
positions and 57 steps.

\subsection{Game corpus}

All probes, rollouts and read-outs stream \texttt{adamkarvonen/othello\_45MB\_games}: 990,623
games (792,498 train, 198,125 test), each exactly 60 plies with no pass token. The corpus is
synthetic uniform-random legal play. Grouping games by $k$-ply prefix and testing each
prefix's children against uniform over the engine-legal set, no prefix with $n \ge 400$ rejects
at $p < 10^{-3}$ for $k = 5$.

Two properties of the corpus condition everything mined from it. First, games are drawn with
\texttt{shuffle(seed=42, buffer\_size=10{,}000)} over a streaming split that is a single shard,
so the shuffle permutes only a 10,000-row reservoir and drawing $N$ games reads the first
$N + 10{,}000$ rows. Every study therefore walks the same leading ${\approx}1.6\%$ of the
corpus in the same order, and their game sets are nested prefixes rather than independent
draws; a different seed replaces ${\approx}80\%$ of the games but stays inside that same slice.
Where two studies must not share games we exclude by exact token match rather than by
reseeding, and say so below. Second, the corpus is lexicographically sorted and truncated to
its 45\,MB byte budget, which cuts one opening: \texttt{e6} holds 10.78\% of games where the
board's symmetry group requires 25\% ($\chi^2 = 85{,}412$ on 3 df). Nothing in the corpus is
illegal and play off that branch is uniform random-legal, but a per-square aggregate can
inherit an asymmetry that has nothing to do with the model.

Forfeits are unmarked in the move stream -- the same color simply moves twice -- so whose turn
it is always comes from engine replay, never from the parity of the move index.

The sparse autoencoders are trained on a separate stream, \texttt{taufeeque/othellogpt}.

\subsection{The illegal-move taxonomy}

Given a board and the player to move, each of the 60 vocabulary squares falls into one of seven
categories: \texttt{legal}, and six violations ordered by how much board computation is needed
to detect them. \textbf{R1 placement} is $\{$\texttt{occupied\_own}, \texttt{occupied\_opp}$\}$;
\textbf{R2 bracketing} is $\{$\texttt{empty\_detached}, \texttt{empty\_own\_only},
\texttt{empty\_no\_bracket}$\}$; \textbf{R3 turn} is $\{$\texttt{opponent\_only}$\}$, a square
that flips only for the player who is not to move. The classifier agrees with the engine's legal
set exactly on non-forfeit positions, which we assert at every position we accept; forfeit
positions are skipped, since there the engine falls back to letting opponent-flipping squares be
played. R2 has three members and R3 has one, so any average over a rule states its $n$.

\subsection{Board-state probes}

Figure~2 uses a tensor product probe (TPR) \citep{lee2025tpr}, $\mathrm{logit}(s, c) = r_s^\top
M(h) f_c$, at role dimension 52 and filler dimension 2 over the 64 squares and the three
mover-relative colors $\{$empty, theirs, mine$\}$: a $[512, 52, 2]$ binding map, a $[64, 52]$
role table and a $[3, 2]$ filler table, 56,582 parameters in total, 57.6\% of the 98,304 of the
per-square linear read-out it is measured against. Architecture and loss are the original
authors'; the recipe is ours, and both probes run behind one protocol so that a head-to-head
compares probe class and nothing else.

Both are fit on 20,000 corpus games at 58 positions each, 1,160,000 rows, split $70/15/15$
\emph{over games} into 812,000 training and 174,000 validation and test rows, with labels from
engine replay. We use AdamW at learning rate $10^{-2}$, batch 8192, at most 400 epochs, stopping
early on validation accuracy with patience 20 and minimum improvement $10^{-4}$, and keep the
best-validation weights. Weight decay is $10^{-4}$ for the linear arm; for the TPR we re-select
it on validation over $\{10^{-4}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-2}, 10^{-1}, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$, which picks
$10^{-3}$ (validation 0.99202, against 0.99107 at 2.0). At layer 5 the held-out test accuracies
are \textbf{0.9920} (TPR, best epoch 31) and \textbf{0.9937} (linear).

\subsection{Legality probes}

Figure~3 uses two linear read-outs of $z_t$ at the position of the candidate move: a binary
$\{$legal, illegal$\}$ head and a four-way $\{$legal, R1, R2, R3$\}$ head.

\textbf{Data.} We mine 800 games at seed 42. An item is a (game, move index) pair carrying the
move actually played plus one substituted illegal move per constraint rule that has a candidate
there; all rows of an item share \texttt{tokens[:$t$]} and differ in exactly one token, so class
is the only thing that varies. The illegal move is drawn uniformly over that rule's candidate
tokens, and positions run over $1 \ldots 58$. The split is $60/20/20$ over games (480/160/160),
giving 27,743 rows per class for the binary head and 23,641 for the four-way head in training.
Balance is structural rather than reweighted: the four-way head keeps only items where all three
rules have a candidate, and the binary head draws one illegal row per item.

\textbf{Fitting.} Multinomial logistic regression with an $\ell_2$ penalty, optimized full-batch
by L-BFGS (300 iterations, strong-Wolfe line search) on features standardized by the training
split's mean and standard deviation. The penalty is chosen from $\{10^{-4}, \ldots, 1\}$ on
validation macro-F1, selecting $10^{-4}$ for both heads. On the held-out test split the binary
head reaches accuracy 0.8896 and AUROC 0.9418, and the four-way head accuracy 0.7760 and
macro-F1 0.7734, with R1 its weakest class (F1 0.6402).

\subsection{Sparse autoencoders}

We compare eight architectures at expansion factor 16 -- 8192 latents on $d_{\rm model} = 512$
-- on layer-5 activations: Standard (ReLU), $p$-Anneal, Gated, Gated+$p$-Anneal, TopK,
BatchTopK, SpaDE and CosineSpaDE.

\textbf{Training.} Our own dictionaries train through one loop off a single activation stream,
so no comparison is confounded by data order: 300M tokens at batch 8192, i.e. 36,621 steps,
learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$ ($2.83\times10^{-4}$ for the TopK family), 1000 warmup steps and
2000 sparsity-warmup steps, at seed 42. Activations are standardized before any autoencoder sees
them, $x' = \sigma (x - \mu)$ with $\mu$ the mean over the first 100 calibration batches and
$\sigma = \sqrt{d_{\rm model} / \mathbb{E}\|x - \mu\|^2}$, so $\mathbb{E}\|x'\|^2 = d_{\rm
model}$. The same transform is applied at encode time. SpaDE scores prototypes by
$-\lambda\|x - Ae_i\|^2$ with $\lambda$ trainable through a softplus, encodes with sparsemax,
and adds a distance-weighted regularizer $\gamma \sum_j \|x - Ad_j\|^2 f_j$; CosineSpaDE scores
by cosine similarity with no $\lambda$ and regularizes with $\sum_j (1 - \cos(x, Ad_j)) f_j$,
which is bounded in $[0, 2]$ because sparsemax sums to one, so its natural $\gamma$ scale is
roughly $500\times$ larger.

\textbf{Matching sparsity.} At each family's own default hyperparameters the eight land anywhere
on the sparsity axis -- their $L_0$ spans 32.5 to 1808 -- so a gap between two spaces confounds
architecture with sparsity. For Figure~5 we instead hold every family at one operating point.
Each type first names its own best-scoring configuration on an equal-weight mean of four
min--max normalized metrics (coverage, reconstruction F$_1$, loss recovered, variance
explained) over a pooled grid of 516 checkpoints at dictionary size 8192; the mean of those
eight $L_0$ values, \textbf{219.16}, becomes a shared target, and each type is then re-seated at
whichever member of its own grid is nearest that target in $\log L_0$ -- by sparsity, not by
score. Realized $L_0$ runs from 183.79 to 260.13, a $1.42\times$ band. CosineSpaDE sits at its
grid maximum: it cannot leave $L_0 \in [19.4, 178.4]$ at any $\gamma$, which is what kept the
target from going higher.

Five of the eight seated checkpoints are Karvonen's released ones \citep{karvonen2025boardgames}
(Standard \texttt{trainer25-v2}, $p$-Anneal \texttt{trainer22}, Gated \texttt{trainer32},
Gated+$p$-Anneal \texttt{trainer25}, TopK \texttt{trainer\_61}) and three are ours (BatchTopK at
$k = 220$, SpaDE at $\lambda = 10^{-3}, \gamma = 5\times10^{-3}$, CosineSpaDE at $\beta = 1,
\gamma = 0.05$). His were trained on the raw residual stream where ours are trained on the
standardized one, so each is encoded under the convention it was trained in, and the two
families' latents are not on one input scale: the roster compares architectures at matched
$L_0$, not one quantity measured eight ways. Matching is on sparsity only -- reconstruction
F$_1$ across the eight still runs 0.464 to 0.938.

\subsection{Figure 1: the rules, and what the model does with them}

\textbf{(a)} The board is game 606 of the seed-42 stream at $t = 16$, black to move. It is not
drawn at random: we scanned $t \in [14, 26]$ over the first 800 games for a position where all
three rules have a candidate, the played move flips discs in more than one direction, and the
four drawn squares stay at least three cells apart. All three rules co-occur at 97.8\% of
positions, so nothing here is rare. The played move b4 is the legal one, and the violations
drawn are \texttt{occupied\_opp} at b2 (R1), \texttt{empty\_no\_bracket} at h3 (R2) and
\texttt{opponent\_only} at f7 (R3). R2 is drawn from its deepest member because the shallowest,
\texttt{empty\_detached}, is an empty square with nothing to see.

\textbf{(b)} We sample 1000 games from the model at temperature 1.0 -- ancestral, no top-$k$ or
nucleus, with the pass token masked -- taking the first move uniformly over the four legal
openings and generating all 58 remaining tokens, at seed 42. Illegal moves are kept rather than
resampled; 885 of the 1000 rollouts are legal throughout. Each rollout is then teacher-forced on
its own tokens, and at every $t \in [0, 56)$ its next-move mass is split by the taxonomy,
keeping only positions where the rollout is still legal through $t$ and the mover does not
forfeit, which leaves 54,766 positions. The curve is the per-game cumulative $1 - \prod_{t' \le
t}(1 - p(t'))$ of that mass, averaged over the games still alive at $t$. Because a game leaves
the population at its first illegal move, $n$ falls from 1000 to 891 across the axis, which is
also why the curves are not monotone. The $y$ axis is logarithmic. Intervals are 95\% percentile
bootstrap over source games, 2000 resamples, seed 0.

This is the probability composed from the model's own next-move distribution, not the rate at
which rollouts actually sample an illegal move; the latter is 11.5\% [9.5, 13.6] by ply 59.

\subsection{Figure 2: bracket flips}

An \emph{event} is a move that flips exactly one disc: for prefix $P = \texttt{tokens[:}t
\texttt{]}$ with mover $e$, the played move $m$ has flip set $\{s\}$, so the board before and
after differ in exactly two squares. Positions where either side forfeits at $t$ or $t+1$ are
dropped, since a forfeit at $t+1$ leaves $e$ to move again and inverts the prediction the study
rests on. The matched control $m'$ is another legal move for the same mover from the same
position that leaves $s$ unflipped and does not forfeit the opponent, so it is matched on
prefix, move index, mover and next mover, and differs only in leaving $s$ alone. A control is
\emph{strict} if its placement and every disc it flips stay off all eight of $s$'s rays, so the
control branch cannot reach $s$ along any line.

\textbf{Read-outs.} From the TPR's three logits at square $s$ we plot $\ell_{\rm mine} -
\ell_{\rm theirs}$, which is positive when the square is owned by the player to move, against
$\tfrac{1}{2}(\ell_{\rm mine} + \ell_{\rm theirs}) - \ell_{\rm empty}$, which is positive when
it is occupied. We read logit differences rather than filler coordinates because the bilinear
form is gauge-invariant under $f \mapsto Af,\; M \mapsto MA^{-1}$: the measured filler angle
moves from $131.3^\circ$ to $104.5^\circ$ across seeds at constant accuracy.

\textbf{(a)} Game 550 at $t = 25$, white to move, with $s = $ c6, $m = $ d7 and $m' = $ g1. It is
picked mechanically rather than at random: among strict-control events at the median move index,
we take the one whose flipped-square displacement is closest to the median displacement over all
2000 events (pool of 34; median 9.566, this event 9.232). It is typical in the measurement, not
in the picture, and $t = 25$ is the corpus median rather than a chosen value.

\textbf{(b)} The panel is a population, not the game beside it: 1000 strict-control events, 40
at each of the 25 move indices $t \in [10, 34]$, sampled at seed 42. We use a fixed quota per
move index rather than whatever the corpus holds, because move index is the strongest nuisance
variable here and it is the window on which Figures~3 and 4 also rest. Meeting that quota needs
a larger mining run -- 4000 events, of which 3194 have a strict control -- from the same miner,
stream and seed. That draw is a separate one, not a superset of the 2000-event corpus every
other measurement uses, so panels (a) and (b) share no events. Each event has exactly one
flipped and one placed square but about 12 unflipped mover's and 13 unflipped opponent's
squares, so the five role markers average over 1000, 1000, 11,935, 13,065 and 37,000
square-observations. Open markers are the state before $m$, filled markers the state after, and
the cross is where $m'$ leaves $s$; the dashed arrow to that cross is the panel's one
contrastive element, and the rest is definitional. Bars are 95\% percentile cluster-bootstrap
intervals over events, 2000 resamples, seed 42, on the \emph{paired} displacement within event,
which is what an arrow asserts -- the two endpoints are strongly anticorrelated on $x$, so an
endpoint interval understates the arrow by about $2\times$ there.

Both axes are read in each condition's own mover-relative frame, and the frame alternates with
the ply, so every unflipped disc crossing $x = 0$ is a consequence of that convention rather
than a finding about the model. The claim the panel supports is the gap between the two heads at
$s$: $13.98$ logits [13.86, 14.10].

Because the probes are fit on 20,000 games of the same seed-42 stream, they have seen most of
the games these events come from. The accuracies quoted above are the probes' own held-out test
numbers, and the panel is a statement about geometry rather than about generalization.

\subsection{Figure 3: probe directions}

\textbf{Directions.} The five pooled directions are read off the two committed probes: from the
four-way head, $\mathrm{unit}(w_i - \bar{w})$ for legal, R1, R2 and R3; from the binary head,
$\mathrm{unit}(w_{\rm illegal} - w_{\rm legal})$. The per-square directions are the same
read-outs refit per candidate move square by ridge regression ($\lambda = 10$) on centered
activations and centered one-hot targets, one point per playable square, so each class
contributes $n = 60$ points. Those refits use all rows of the fit set, so panel (a) describes
the geometry of the fit rather than a held-out one. Note that the ring in (a) is the pooled
committed probe, a separate fit on pooled rows, not the centroid of its own points.

\textbf{(a)} Two-dimensional non-metric MDS (SMACOF, deterministic classical-scaling
initialization, one restart, 1000 iterations) on the matrix of geodesic angles $\theta_{ij} =
\arccos(x_i^\top x_j)$ between unit-normalized directions; we use the angle rather than $1 -
\cos$ because it is a metric. Per-square points and pooled directions are embedded in one fit,
and the orientation is pinned by orthogonal Procrustes onto the PCA coordinates so a refit
cannot rotate or mirror the panel. The embedding preserves family membership but not finer
structure -- its rank fidelity, 0.504, sits below a structure-free null of $0.574 \pm 0.007$ --
so the panel should be read for which families separate, and angles should be read off (b).

\textbf{(b)} Cosine similarities among the five pooled directions. In $\mathbb{R}^{512}$ random
unit directions meet at $|\cos| \approx 0.035$, which is the scale these entries should be read
against.

\textbf{(c)} AUROC of each head on the spliced branch tree of \S9, which is disjoint from the
800 probe-fit games by exact token match. Each illegal branch is scored against the same source
game's legal control at the same $t_0$, over the same 500-game evaluation set the detector uses,
so these bars and the detector's population panels score one set of games rather than two built
by the same recipe. The score is $1 - P(\text{legal})$, taken from the binary head for the first
bar and from the four-way posterior for the rule bars. AUROC is rank-based with ties averaged,
and bars carry 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals over game pairs, 1000 resamples, seed 42. The
chance line at 0.5 is licensed empirically: scoring a game's legal control against the second
legal control it carries gives 0.4875 [0.4587, 0.5144].

\subsection{Figure 4: the detector on residual-stream activations}

\textbf{The branch tree.} Each source game is forked once at $t_0$ into nine branches that share
\texttt{tokens[:$t_0$]} and \texttt{tokens[$t_0$+1:]} and differ only at $t_0$: the move actually
played, two further legal moves, and one move per available illegal category, drawn uniformly
within its category at seed 42. There are two legal controls rather than one because a second is
what supplies the legal-versus-legal null. Nothing is regenerated after $t_0$ -- the suffix is
kept verbatim -- so a difference downstream is attributable to that one token and to nothing
else. Fork points are round-robin over $t_0 \in [10, 34]$, and a later pass appended games forked
at $t_0 = 20$. The tree holds 3923 games and 34,546 branches.

\textbf{Populations.} We score every branch-complete game with $t_0 \in [10, 34]$ whose source
game has odd parity in the legal corpus below: 2415 games, 21,735 branch rows. The per-move-index
profile $\mu_t, \sigma_t$ is fit on a block of 2000 legal games, minus the 778 that are
themselves scored here -- a trajectory must not contribute to the baseline it is scored against
-- leaving \textbf{1222}. The evaluation set for the population panels is 500 games, an equal
quota of 20 at each of the 25 values of $t_0$, taken as the first by sorted game id. The tree is
not balanced on move index, forking 1665 of the eligible games at $t_0 = 20$, and move index is
the strongest nuisance variable it has, which is what the quota removes. Those 500 games are
written down once and re-read by Figures~3(c) and 5 rather than re-dealt.

\textbf{The statistic.} With the sink dropped a game yields 57 steps, and the candidate move
index is the step index plus two, so an $m = 4$ window reaches moves 6--55: 50 candidates.
Equations (1)--(3) are computed exactly as written, with $g_t$ the negative of the $z$-score so
that a \emph{shorter}-than-usual step scores high.

\textbf{(a, b)} Game 3889 at $t_0 = 20$. It is chosen so that all three rules have a branch
there, $t_0 \pm 5$ stays inside the scanned band, the score peaks at $t_0$ on all three rule arms
while the matched legal control does not, and the detector is right about at least four of the
five plies drawn; among those, the largest margin over its own runner-up. The last two are
selections over games, so this is an illustrative game rather than a typical one, and the
population is panels (c) and (d). Off $t_0$ the row scored is the reference game, whose move
there is the legal one it played, so anything above $\tau$ is a false alarm; at $t_0$ it is the
drawn branch, and the rule members drawn are \texttt{occupied\_opp}, \texttt{empty\_no\_bracket}
and \texttt{opponent\_only}. The shaded region is $t \le t_0 - m$, where all four branches are
still bit-identical because the window has not yet reached the splice. After $t_0$ the branch
replays the source game's suffix onto a board that no longer exists, so the right half shows the
score decaying and is not a legality judgement of those moves.

\textbf{(c)} AUROC of $S$ at each row's own $t_0$ -- one value per branch, not a maximum over the
window -- for the illegal branch against the same game's legal control. A rule arm is one branch
per game drawn at seed 42, never a mean over its member categories, so R2 with three members
carries the same estimator as R3 with one. Intervals are 95\% percentile bootstrap over source
games, 1000 resamples, seed 42.

\textbf{(d)} Gaussian kernel density estimates (Scott's rule) rescaled to a count axis on one
slice width shared across all five series and across spaces, so a curve's height is comparable
between panels. The threshold $\tau = 0.618$ is the cut maximizing balanced accuracy, found by an
exact sweep of the breakpoints and refit on this population, pooling every scored position up to
$t_0$ across all four branch blocks with positives only at $t_0$ of the illegal arm. It gives TPR
0.658 and TNR 0.683. The objective is nearly flat -- balanced accuracy is 0.6644 at $\tau = 0.869$
-- so $\tau$ names a trade-off rather than a sharp optimum, and 37\% of legal controls sit above
it.
